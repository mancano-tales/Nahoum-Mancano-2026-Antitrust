### **[SLIDE 2 — The puzzle]**  (~0:00)

Let me start with something a Brazilian finance minister, Pedro Malan, said in the 1990s: *"the best industrial policy is to have no industrial policy."* And he meant it. Under President Cardoso, Brazil was liberalizing — opening trade, privatizing, fighting inflation, getting the state out of the business of picking winners.

And yet, in those same years, that same government helped assemble some of the largest mergers in the country's history — in steel, and in beer — and defended them, in public, as necessary to build Brazilian firms strong enough to compete in the world.

So here is my puzzle: how does a government run an industrial policy while sincerely denying that it has one? My answer, in one sentence — not through subsidies, and not through protection, because there was no money for either, but through the way it *enforced its competition law*. An industrial policy that cost the Treasury nothing.

### **[SLIDE 3 — The argument]**  (~1:10)

So the claim is this. Brazil's flexible antitrust enforcement in the 1990s worked as a *de facto* industrial policy — it forged national champions that became multinationals. My evidence is two government-backed mergers: Gerdau, in steel; and Antarctica and Brahma merging into Ambev, in beer. The mechanism is what Mahoney and Thelen call *institutional conversion*: the law on the books never changes — what changes is how it's read. Watch for that as we go: nothing here is illegal, and almost nothing is even hidden.

### **[SLIDE 4 — The received view]**  (~1:50)

Why is that surprising? Because for a century, antitrust and industrial policy were treated as opposites. Antitrust is the referee: it doesn't care who wins, only that nobody rigs the game. Industrial policy is the coach: it picks a team and helps it win. On the standard view — from Brandeis all the way to Chicago — a state does one or the other.

But that line was always porous. Dobbin and Prasad showed that the referee's rulebook was never neutral: which industries grew in the early United States depended on how the rules of competition were written and enforced — no ministry of industry required. So the real question isn't *whether* the line can be crossed. It's *how* a government crosses it when it has publicly promised not to.

### **[SLIDE 5 — Economization: form vs. effect]**  (~2:45)

To see how, I need to give you one idea — the only piece of theory you'll need. It's from Ergen and Kohl. Since the 1960s, competition regimes everywhere were "economized" — they dropped their old social goals and kept only economic ones. But in two opposite ways.

Here's the intuition. A *form-based* regime is like a speed limit: cross the line and you're guilty, full stop — no argument about whether you were actually driving dangerously. An *effect-based* regime asks the other question: *was* your driving actually harmful? — and now you get to argue. Empty road, clear weather, skilled driver.

Only the second one requires a judgment call in every case. And every judgment call is a place the decision could have gone either way — call those *discretionary joints*. A form-based regime has basically one: where you draw the boundary of the market. An effect-based regime has three: the market, the predicted effects, and whether the claimed efficiencies count. More joints, more room for pressure to enter — without anyone breaking a rule. Hold onto that: it's the whole difference between my two cases.

### **[SLIDE 6–8 — Context]**  (~3:50)  ⏩ *(cut if tight — say the two bold sentences and move on)*

One quick piece of context. It's tempting to hear "1990s liberalization" and picture the state shrinking and walking away. **That's wrong, and my argument depends on it.** Cardoso broke with the old military-era model — import substitution, state firms, protected markets — but he didn't abolish the state; he rebuilt it into a *regulatory* state, with independent agencies and technocrats. **The competition authority, CADE, is a child of that moment** — strengthened in 1994, and brand new: no entrenched professional community behind it. Remember that — discretion, with nothing yet to discipline it.

### **[SLIDE 9 — Gerdau: the rebars of discord]**  (~4:30)

The first case. Gerdau, a steelmaker, buys the Brazilian assets of a German group. Most of it is uncontroversial. The fight is about one product — rebar, the steel rods in every construction site — where the deal would give Gerdau close to half the national market. And the decisive fact: the 1994 law still carried one hard, form-based number — a twenty-percent threshold. Gerdau would be at forty-six.

### **[SLIDE 10 — Gerdau timeline]**  (~5:05)

So CADE says no — it orders the concentrating part undone. And Gerdau does something remarkable. It doesn't go to court. It goes to the Minister of Justice, who suspends CADE's decision — even though the law says, in black and white, that the executive cannot review CADE. And then the councillors who voted no are replaced. Only three years later does a reshaped CADE approve the deal. The next year, the champion goes global, buying into the United States.

### **[SLIDE 11 — Gerdau: why brute force]**  (~5:45)

Why did it take all that — overriding the law and purging the agency? Because there was only *one* soft joint to work. With a hard twenty-percent line, there's nothing to weigh — the number just decides. The only thing left to argue was the market. If the market is "steel in Brazil," Gerdau is enormous; if it's "steel in the world," Gerdau is small — same company, same mills, and the answer flips with where you draw the line. But CADE had the evidence: Brazilian prices had come loose from world prices, so this really was a national market. Gerdau lost on the merits. So winning took brute force, in public. Remember that — the second case looks completely different.

### **[SLIDE 12 — Ambev: the green-and-yellow multinational]**  (~6:40)

Beer. Through the 1990s the global brewers were circling Brazil's two biggest — Antarctica and Brahma. So the two merged, into a self-declared "green-and-yellow multinational" — a national champion in so many words. And notice the fiscal twist: Brazilian credit was so expensive that a foreign buyer could always outbid a domestic one. Building scale at home was the only affordable defense — which is exactly why the help these firms needed had to be non-fiscal.

### **[SLIDE 13 — Ambev: a public war]**  (~7:20)

And this was not quiet. The merger is announced at the presidential palace, with Cardoso between the two founders. CADE suspends it — but the companies are already operating as one, daring the agency to unscramble the egg. A rival, Kaiser, runs three advertising campaigns against it. CADE's own technical staff revolt: the promised efficiencies collapse from fourteen percent to under seven, and a police bribery investigation even brushes the council. Ministers phone CADE's president. The development minister publicly offers three billion reais of state-bank money to build champions. The President says, on the record, "the world runs on oligopoly." One councillor votes no. And it passes anyway.

### **[SLIDE 14 — Ambev: the effect-based regime at work]**  (~8:20)

Here's the paradox — and it's the heart of the paper. This was a *louder* war than Gerdau: more public, more political. And yet nobody had to break a law. No minister overrode anything; no one was purged. Why? Because now the regime had three joints, not one. Ambev argued the market was "all beverages," and the rivalry global — against Coca-Cola. It argued efficiencies. Each was arguable. So the same pressure that in Gerdau took an illegal override was, in Ambev, simply *absorbed by the reasoning*. The statute never changed a word — and the outcome changed completely. That is what institutional conversion looks like from the inside.

### **[SLIDE 15 — Discussion: the two cases compared]**  (~9:05)

Side by side, then. Gerdau: one hard joint, so it took open conflict and an illegal override. Ambev: three soft joints, so identical pressure passed as routine. The difference isn't that the actors got tired — it's how much room the doctrine gave them. And let me concede one thing before you raise it: this is two cases. Ergen and Kohl are right that this kind of change happens piece by piece. So what I've shown is a *repertoire that became available* — not a regime permanently installed.

### **[SLIDE 16 — Brazil's contribution]**  (~9:40)

What's new here? Three things, and an irony. First, the crux is *public denial* — not a discreet government, but one that officially rejected the whole category of industrial policy. Second, that denial makes the policy *illegible*: read CADE's decisions cold and they look like ordinary consumer-welfare antitrust — the developmental purpose is simply not in the text, which is why you have to trace it through the case files. Third, it's *fiscally free*: the state built a champion by *not* blocking a merger — the cheapest industrial policy there is, and the only kind available under fiscal discipline. And the irony: the case-by-case toolkit was invented in Chicago to keep the state *out* of the economy. In Brazil, it's exactly what let the state *in*.

### **[SLIDE 17 — Industrial policy: the normality of denial]**  (~10:35)  ⏩ *(cut if tight — fold to one line: "and denial, by the way, is normal — the leading scholars of industrial policy say the selective part is usually left implicit")*

You might think: isn't this just Brazil being Brazil? No. The leading economists of industrial policy today — Juhász, Lane and Rodrik — define it as deliberately targeting the structure of the economy, and they note that the selective part, "we help this but not that," is *typically left implicit*. In developing countries, they say, the very term is avoided for its bad reputation. Denial is normal. Brazil isn't the exception — it's the pure case.

### **[SLIDE 18 — Three fates of administrative discretion]**  (~11:00)

And this is where it speaks to antitrust beyond Brazil. Foster and Thelen show that what decides how a competition regime behaves is how much discretion the regulator has — and what disciplines it. Germany disciplined its discretion with rules, and stayed form-based. The European Union disciplined it with slow institutional learning, and — surprisingly — ended up closest to the old Brandeisian ideal of regulated competition. Brazil had the discretion and nothing yet to discipline it — so an executive could lean on a two-year-old agency, and the discretion converted into a covert industrial policy. Same lever; three different fates. Brazil is the cell comparative antitrust was missing.

### **[SLIDE 19 — Thank you]**  (~11:45)

So let me leave you with one line. The case-by-case toolkit built to keep the state *out* of the economy is exactly what, in Brazil, let it *in* — for free, and under public denial. Thank you.
