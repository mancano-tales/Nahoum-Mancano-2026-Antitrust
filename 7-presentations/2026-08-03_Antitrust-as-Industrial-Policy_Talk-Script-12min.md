### **[SLIDE 1 — Presentation]**  (~0:00)

Hello, I'm Tales Mançano — a graduate student in Political Science at the University of São Paulo. Today I'll be presenting our paper, *Antitrust as Industrial Policy: Government-Sponsored Mergers as Passive Industrial Policy in Brazil, 1995–2002*.

### **[SLIDE 2 — The puzzle]**  (~0:00)

Let me start with a famous line from Brazil's finance minister of the period, Pedro Malan: *"the best industrial policy is to have no industrial policy."* Under President Cardoso, who took office in 1995, Brazil was liberalizing — opening trade, privatizing state-owned enterprises, fighting inflation, and pulling the state out of the business of protecting and running companies. The official discourse was that industrial policy was over.

At the same time, Brazil's antitrust law was not new — a competition authority, CADE, had existed since 1962, modeled on the American agencies — but it acquired genuine enforcement capacity only in 1994, when a new statute refounded it as an independent regulator whose decisions, by law, the Executive could not overturn.

And yet, in those very same years, that same government intervened in CADE's proceedings to secure the approval of some of the largest mergers in the country's history — in steel, and in beer — and defended them, in public, as necessary to build Brazilian firms strong enough to compete in a globalized market.

So here is my puzzle: how does a government run an industrial policy while sincerely denying that it has one?

Let me put our working definition of industrial policy on the table up front — one we arrived at through the literature: *"government policies that explicitly target the transformation of the structure of economic activity in pursuit of some public goal … A key characteristic is the exercise of choice and discretion by the public authorities — 'we promote X but not Y' — though the latter part is typically left implicit."* Hold on to that last clause — "typically left implicit" — because it is exactly what our case makes visible.

And to be clear, I'm not saying Malan was lying or posturing. His conception of industrial policy was simply a different one — he had in mind tariffs and subsidies, the classic tools. Our point is that industrial policy can also be pursued *without* subsidies and *without* protective tariffs — through the *forbearance* of competition law. That makes it fiscally cheap, which matters enormously for a government under fiscal constraints, and it can happen in the middle of a so-called liberalization.

### **[SLIDE 3 — The argument]**  (~1:10)

So the claim is that the forbearance of antitrust enforcement in the 1990s worked as a *de facto* industrial policy — it forged national champions that went on to become multinationals. My evidence is two government-backed mergers: Gerdau, in steel; and Antarctica and Brahma merging into Ambev, in beer. The mechanism is what Mahoney and Thelen call *institutional conversion*: the law on the books never changes — what changes is how it is read, above all through the definition of the relevant market, which let firms merge on terms that an earlier reading would not have accepted. And note, as we go: nothing here is illegal, and none of the evidence I show was ever secret.

### **[SLIDE 4 — The received view]**  (~1:50)

Why is that surprising? Because, on the conventional, maybe already superada, account, antitrust and industrial policy were thought to embody opposite institutional logics. Antitrust is a rule-based order: general prohibitions, applied impersonally and adjudicated before a competition tribunal, indifferent in principle to which firms prevail. Industrial policy is a discretionary order: the selective, often ad hoc, promotion of particular sectors or firms. One disciplines the market; the other steers it. From the Brandeisian tradition to the Chicago School — for all they otherwise disagree — both treat the two as mutually exclusive.

But that line was always blurred. A long literature shows that antitrust enforcement was never neutral — that how competition rules are written and applied shapes the structure of the economy in ways that look very much like industrial policy. So the real question isn't *whether* the line can be crossed. It's *how* a government crosses it when it doesn't think it is doing so.

### **[SLIDE 5 — Economization: form vs. effect]**  (~2:45)

To see how, one analytical distinction two divergent institutional forms that antitrust took around the world. 
In a form-based regime — the ordoliberal model of which Germany is the main case — illegality attaches to market structure and conduct as such: it operates through bright-line rules and structural presumptions, and asks little about the concrete effects of a given transaction, it doens belive the state has to take in account the effect of concetrantion acts. In an effect-based regime — the Chicago model — no amount of market concentration is unlawful in itself; a practice is condemned only where a net harm to consumer-welfare can be demonstrated, case by case, weighing efficiencies against anticompetitive effects.

The consequence that matters for us is that the effect-based approach multiplies the points of discretion. Where a form-based regime turns on essentially one contestable determination — the definition of the relevant market — an effect-based regime opens three: the market, the prediction of competitive effects, and the assessment of claimed efficiencies. Each could reasonably be resolved either way, and is therefore a point at which external pressure can enter with no formal breach of the law. How many such determinations a regime requires is precisely what separates my two cases.

### **[SLIDE 9 — Gerdau: the rebars of discord]**  (~4:30)

The first case. Gerdau, a steelmaker, buys the Brazilian assets of a German group Korpf. The dispute is about one product — rebar, the steel rods in every construction site — where the deal would give Gerdau close to half the national market. And the decisive fact: the 1994 law still carried one hard, form-based number — a twenty-percent threshold. Gerdau would be at forty-six.

### **[SLIDE 10 — Gerdau timeline]**  (~5:05)

So CADE says no — it orders the concentrating part of the deal undone. And Gerdau does something remarkable. It does not go to court. It appeals to the Minister of Justice, who suspends CADE's decision — even though the law states explicitly that the executive cannot review CADE. Many councillors are replaced. Only three years later does a reconstituted CADE approve the deal. The next year, the champion goes global, acquiring its way into the United States.

### **[SLIDE 11 — Gerdau: why brute force]**  (~5:45)

The only thing left to argue was the market. If the market is "steel in Brazil," Gerdau is enormous; if it is "steel in the world," Gerdau is small — same company, same mills, and the answer reverses depending on where you draw the line. But CADE had the evidence: Brazilian prices had decoupled from world prices, so this really was a national market. Gerdau lost on the merits. So winning required open, extra-legal force. Remember that — the second case looks completely different.

### **[SLIDE 12 — Ambev: the green-and-yellow multinational]**  (~6:40)

Beer. Through the 1990s the global brewers were courting Brazil's two biggest — Antarctica and Brahma. So the two merged, into a self-declared "green-and-yellow multinational" — a national champion in so many words. And there is a fiscal point worth noting: Brazilian credit was so expensive that a foreign buyer could always outbid a domestic one. Building scale at home was the only affordable response — which is exactly why the help these firms needed had to be non-fiscal.

### **[SLIDE 13 — Ambev: a public war]**  (~7:20)

And this was not quiet. The merger is announced at the presidential palace, with Cardoso between the two founders. CADE suspends it — but the firms are already operating as one, presenting the agency with a *fait accompli* it could not realistically undo. A rival, Kaiser, runs three advertising campaigns against it. CADE's own technical staff dissent: the promised efficiencies collapse from fourteen percent to under seven, and a police bribery investigation reaches the council. Ministers phone CADE's president. The development minister publicly offers three billion reais of state-bank money to build champions. The President says, on the record, "the world runs on oligopoly." One councillor votes no. And it passes anyway.

### **[SLIDE 14 — Ambev: the effect-based regime at work]**  (~8:20)

Here's the paradox — and it's the heart of the argument. This was a *louder* fight than Gerdau: more public, more political. And yet nobody had to break a law. No minister overrode anything; no one was replaced. Why? Because now the regime had three points of discretion, not one. Ambev argued the market was "all beverages," and the rivalry global — against Coca-Cola. It argued efficiencies. Each was genuinely arguable. So the same pressure that in Gerdau required an illegal override was, in Ambev, simply absorbed within the reasoning. The statute never changed a word — and the outcome changed completely. That is what institutional conversion looks like from the inside.

### **[SLIDE 15 — Discussion: the two cases compared]**  (~9:05)

Side by side, then. Gerdau: a bright-line threshold and a single open point of discretion, so it took open conflict and an illegal override. Ambev: three open points of discretion, so identical pressure passed as routine. The difference is not actor fatigue — it is how much interpretive room the doctrine afforded. And let me concede one thing before you raise it: this is two cases. Ergen and Kohl are right that this kind of change happens piecemeal. So what I have shown is a *repertoire that became available* — not a regime permanently installed.

### **[SLIDE 16 — Brazil's contribution]**  (~9:40)

What is new here? Three things, and an irony. First, the crux is *public denial* — not a discreet government, but one that officially rejected the whole category of industrial policy. Second, that denial makes the policy *illegible*: read CADE's decisions on their own terms and they look like ordinary consumer-welfare antitrust — the developmental purpose is simply not in the text, which is why you have to reconstruct it from the case files. Third, it is *fiscally free*: the state built a champion by *not* blocking a merger — the cheapest industrial policy there is, and the only kind available under fiscal discipline. And the irony: the case-by-case apparatus was devised in Chicago to keep the state *out* of the economy. In Brazil, it is precisely what let the state *in*.

### **[SLIDE 17 — Industrial policy: the normality of denial]**  (~10:35)  ⏩ *(cut if tight — fold to one line: "and denial, by the way, is normal — the leading scholars of industrial policy say the selective part is usually left implicit")*

You might ask: isn't this just a Brazilian idiosyncrasy? No. The leading economists of industrial policy today — Juhász, Lane and Rodrik — define it as the deliberate targeting of the economy's structure, and they observe that the selective part, "we help this but not that," is *typically left implicit*. In developing countries, they note, the very term is avoided for its negative connotation. Denial is normal. Brazil is not the exception — it is the pure case.

### **[SLIDE 18 — Three fates of administrative discretion]**  (~11:00)

And this is where it speaks to antitrust beyond Brazil. Foster and Thelen show that what determines how a competition regime behaves is how much discretion the regulator holds — and what disciplines that discretion. Germany disciplined it with rules, and remained form-based. The European Union disciplined it through slow institutional learning and — strikingly — ended up closest to the old Brandeisian ideal of regulated competition. Brazil had the discretion and nothing yet to discipline it — so the executive could exert pressure on a two-year-old agency, and the discretion was converted into a covert industrial policy. The same variable — discretion — yields three different outcomes. Brazil is the case comparative antitrust had not yet accounted for.

### **[SLIDE 19 — Thank you]**  (~11:45)

So let me leave you with one line. The case-by-case apparatus built to keep the state *out* of the economy is exactly what, in Brazil, let it *in* — for free, and under public denial. Thank you.
